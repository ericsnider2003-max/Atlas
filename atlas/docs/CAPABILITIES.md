# What Atlas can do

Generated by `atlas catalog --markdown`. Do not edit by hand — a hand-kept copy of this drifted from the code for a week, and a document about what a system does is worth nothing the moment it stops being the system's own answer.

Ask it directly — *"what can you do?"*, *"what's new?"*, *"what are you waiting on?"*, *"can you read my email?"* — or read this.

**State** is what Atlas has written. The platform columns are what the platform would allow if it had. The two are separate on purpose: something can be impossible on an iPhone and unbuilt everywhere, and collapsing them hides which.

| state | means |
|---|---|
| **working** | you can rely on it today |
| **switched off** | built and tested, waiting for you to turn it on |
| **waiting on something** | needs something installed |
| **built, never run for real** | compiles and passes tests, has never touched real hardware |
| **not built yet** | nothing in the running program can reach it |

| platform | means |
|---|---|
| ready | the platform allows it, and Atlas's layer for that platform is written |
| would | the platform allows it; that layer isn't written yet |
| catch | allowed, with a compromise worth knowing — listed at the bottom |
| **no** | the platform forbids it, however much gets written |

There is one build of Atlas, and it is the Windows one. Every other column is what that platform permits, not something you can install today.

## What a platform will never allow

**Windows** — nothing. The rest is time.

**macOS** — nothing. The rest is time.

**Linux** — nothing. The rest is time.

**iOS**

- **moving your windows** — there are no windows to arrange — one app fills the screen, and the system decides when two share it. So: arrange your windows; put a panel on screen when you ask; type on your desktop with no window; open your workspace or a named mode -- trading, writing, a call -- each app on its screen, and close it all again.
- **reading your screen** — one app cannot see or touch another. Shortcuts is the sanctioned way round it and it only does what each app chose to expose. So: keep where your time went — which app, for how long, your longest stretch of focus, and what you were in the middle of when you come back from a break — on this machine only; read text off an image with an outside program; read the words on your screen; notice what window you're in; copy the text off the window in front, read on this machine by Windows' own recognizer, and warn you if it holds something secret; keep a receipt off the screen or the clipboard -- merchant, date, total, asked about when unsure -- and say what you spent where; explain or answer about whatever you copied -- and when nothing is copied, work out what "this" is from what you were looking at; go and look at another window to answer you, then put your focus back where it was.
- **doing things inside other apps** — one app cannot see or touch another. Shortcuts is the sanctioned way round it and it only does what each app chose to expose. So: type what you say into a window; drive a browser; fix your typing as you go; do something that crosses several apps; draft a reply in the window in front, or carry the conversation on while you're away — typing only in gaps in yours, and never sending a reply that didn't land as written; type a two-factor code you read out or that's in your email or texts, sign you in and make accounts in its own browser, and turn two-factor on or off after reading it back; fix a typo in place as you type in your other apps, leave a fix you changed back alone in that text, and learn from what you keep and what you change; keep text you type often and type it for you, by voice or a key chord, without ever watching your typing; read an open window's controls and text without a screenshot -- even one that isn't in front -- and press a button you name.
- **hearing you without opening it** — an app that isn't in front of you gets no microphone. This is the platform's decision and it isn't going to change. So: hear a wake word and listen; wake on your own phrase, taught from three recordings, without speech-to-text running all day.
