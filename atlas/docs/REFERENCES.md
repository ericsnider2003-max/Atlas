# What other fictional systems got right

You asked me to look at how this has been done before. Below is what I took
from each, and — more usefully — what each one got wrong.

---

## The one that solves your actual complaint: KITT

*Knight Rider, 1982.* A horizontal bar of segments that moves with the voice.

It is forty years old and it is still the clearest solution to the problem you
raised. **You can tell, instantly and without thinking, whether it is talking.**
A ring going round tells you nothing — Windows uses one for copying files. A
voice form is unmistakably a voice.

**Taken:** the mark is now a voice signature rather than a spinner. Flat when
idle, moving with the actual audio when speaking, and a slow asymmetric wave
when thinking — which reads as clearly *not* speech, so the two never get
confused.

## HAL 9000

*2001, 1968.* One unblinking red eye. Never moves.

The lesson is the opposite of what people copy from it. HAL is unsettling
because it is **still** — presence without motion. Most assistant interfaces
animate constantly to prove they are alive, which is exactly what makes them
feel like software.

**Taken:** the idle state barely moves. The thing in the corner of your screen
all day is nearly static, and motion means something is actually happening.

**Not taken:** the eye. A watching eye is the wrong metaphor for something that
should feel like it works for you.

## TARS

*Interstellar, 2014.* A featureless slab with a small text display. No face, no
orb, no glow.

TARS has more personality than any of the others and it has no visual identity
at all. It comes entirely from **how it talks** — dry, direct, and it argues
with you. There is a scene where its honesty setting is adjusted, which is a
better idea than most interface design.

**Taken:** more than anything else on this list. Atlas's character lives in the
persona rules — brief, dry, disagrees, never flatters — and there are settings
for exactly that. Personality through language costs nothing to render.

## Samantha

*Her, 2013.* No visual presence whatsoever. An earpiece and a folding device
with a lens.

The most radical choice on the list: the interface is the conversation. When
she appears on screen at all it is a small warm light.

**Taken:** the panels are transient. They appear when summoned, say their
piece, and leave. Nothing sits on your screen demanding to be looked at.

## Cortana

*Halo, 2001.* A figure standing on a data plinth, rendered in shifting blue.

The useful part is not the figure — it is that her rendering **degrades
visibly** as she deteriorates. State is legible without a status bar.

**Taken:** the mark's state is the mark, not a label next to it. Nothing says
"THINKING" in small caps.

## JARVIS

*Iron Man, 2008.* Layered translucent panels, concentric rings, everything
cyan.

Honestly the weakest reference visually, and the most copied. The layered
translucency is genuinely good — information at several depths, foreground
sharp, background dimmed. The cyan is a cliché the moment you reach for it.

**Taken:** depth. Panels sit over your desktop with the thing behind darkened
rather than hidden.

**Not taken:** cyan. Mint is close enough to feel related, far enough to not be
a costume.

## MU-TH-UR and the Nostromo terminals

*Alien, 1979.* Green phosphor on black, monospace, deliberately slow.

The typing is the whole point — text arriving character by character reads as
something being *said*.

**Taken:** the waking overlay types itself in, with a beat on every full stop.

**Not taken:** the monospace and the green. That is a period costume now, not a
design.

## GLaDOS and EDI

*Portal, 2007; Mass Effect, 2010.* Both prove a system can be a character with
almost no visual vocabulary — an eye on an arm, a blue orb.

**Taken:** confidence in restraint. Neither has a dashboard.

---

## Where they all fail, and what that tells us

Every one of these is a **film prop**. None has to be looked at eight hours a
day while you work, and none has to tell you anything you can act on. That is
why they are all more theatrical than useful, and why copying their look
directly produces something that feels like a costume.

The three ideas worth stealing are all functional rather than decorative:

1. **KITT's voice form** — state you can read without thinking.
2. **TARS's character** — personality from language, not pixels.
3. **HAL's stillness** — motion means something happened.

Everything else on the list is set dressing.

---

## What none of them do, and Atlas should

- **Show its reasoning.** Every one of these is a black box that announces
  conclusions. Being able to watch the thinking is more impressive than any
  amount of glow, and it is the thing that makes it yours rather than magic.
- **Admit uncertainty.** None of them ever say "I don't know."
- **Track what you're actually doing.** They respond; they don't hold a picture
  of your projects and where each one stands.

Those three are worth more than the entire visual language above, and they are
the ones I would spend effort on.
